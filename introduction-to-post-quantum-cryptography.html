Introduction to Post-Quantum Cryptography

Almost every secure connection on the internet relies on two mathematical problems that a large enough quantum computer could solve. This article explains why, what is replacing today's public-key algorithms, and what organizations should be doing about it now. It is based on the research I carried out for my bachelor's thesis.

The problem in one paragraph

Public-key cryptography is what lets two parties who have never met agree on a secret key, and what lets a server prove its identity with a certificate. In practice it rests on two families of algorithms: RSA, whose security depends on how hard it is to factor a large number into its two primes, and elliptic-curve cryptography (ECDH, ECDSA, Ed25519), which depends on the hardness of the discrete logarithm problem. For classical computers both problems are, for well-chosen parameters, out of reach. For a sufficiently large, error-corrected quantum computer they are not.

What Shor's algorithm breaks

In 1994 Peter Shor showed that a quantum computer can factor integers and compute discrete logarithms in polynomial time. It is worth being precise here: factoring and the discrete logarithm are different mathematical problems. What they have in common is that both can be reduced to finding the period of a function, and period-finding is exactly what a quantum computer does well.

For factoring, the idea is to pick a random a and look at the function f(x) = aˣ mod N. That function repeats with some period r. Classically, finding r takes exponential time. A quantum computer puts the input register in a superposition of all values of x, evaluates f on all of them at once, and then applies the Quantum Fourier Transform, which makes the amplitudes interfere so that measurement returns information about r with high probability. The QFT does not check a candidate period; it extracts it from the interference pattern.

Once the period is known, the rest is classical arithmetic. The textbook example with N = 15:

# N = 15, choose a = 7 (coprime with 15)
7¹ mod 15 = 7
7² mod 15 = 4
7³ mod 15 = 13
7⁴ mod 15 = 1      # the sequence repeats → period r = 4

# r is even, so compute a^(r/2) = 7² = 49
gcd(49 − 1, 15) = gcd(48, 15) = 3
gcd(49 + 1, 15) = gcd(50, 15) = 5

# 15 = 3 × 5 — the private key of a (toy) RSA modulus is recovered

With real 2048-bit moduli the quantum part is enormously harder to build, but the logic is identical. The same machinery applied to the discrete logarithm breaks Diffie-Hellman and every elliptic-curve scheme in use today. Unlike most attacks, this one cannot be fixed by using bigger keys: Shor's algorithm scales polynomially, so key sizes would have to grow impractically large.

What Grover's algorithm weakens

Symmetric cryptography (AES, ChaCha20) and hash functions (SHA-2, SHA-3) are in a much better position. The relevant quantum attack is Grover's algorithm, which speeds up brute-force search quadratically: searching 2ⁿ keys takes roughly 2ⁿᐟ² quantum operations. In theory, AES-128 drops to about 64 bits of security.

The fix is simply to compensate with larger parameters: AES-256 keeps a comfortable margin even against Grover, and in practice Grover is also hard to parallelize, so the real-world speedup is smaller than the theoretical one. Symmetric crypto needs tuning, not replacement. The real emergency is public-key crypto.

Why it matters before quantum computers exist

No quantum computer today can break RSA-2048. So why act now? Because of "harvest now, decrypt later": an attacker can record encrypted traffic today and store it until a cryptographically relevant quantum computer becomes available. Any data that must stay confidential for many years (health records, state secrets, intellectual property, long-term credentials) is already exposed.

Michele Mosca summarized the decision with a simple inequality:

x + y > z ⇒ you are already late

where x is how long your data must stay secret, y is how long your migration will take, and z is how long until a large quantum computer exists. Nobody knows z precisely, but x and y are often measured in many years, and large organizations historically need a decade or more to replace a cryptographic algorithm everywhere.

Post-quantum is not quantum cryptography

The two terms are often confused, and the difference is important:

  • Quantum cryptography (for example Quantum Key Distribution, QKD) uses quantum physics to exchange keys. It needs dedicated hardware such as fiber links or satellites, and it does not solve authentication on its own.
  • Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) is ordinary software running on ordinary computers. It uses new mathematical problems that are believed to be hard even for quantum computers. It can be deployed over the existing internet, in TLS, VPNs, SSH and code signing.

When security teams talk about "the quantum migration", they almost always mean PQC.

The mathematical families behind PQC

Post-quantum schemes are grouped by the hard problem they rely on:

  • Lattice-based: problems such as Learning With Errors (LWE) and its structured variants. Fast, with reasonably small keys; the basis of most new standards.
  • Hash-based signatures: security reduces to the properties of a hash function, which is the most conservative assumption available. The trade-off is larger signatures.
  • Code-based: based on decoding random error-correcting codes, an idea dating back to McEliece in 1978 and very well studied.
  • Multivariate and isogeny-based schemes: smaller ecosystems, with several notable breaks.
Lesson learned

In 2022 Castryck and Decru broke SIKE, an isogeny-based finalist, in about an hour on a single classical laptop. It was not a quantum attack at all. New hardness assumptions need years of public cryptanalysis, which is why standards bodies favor diversity and conservative designs.

The NIST standards

After an open competition that started in 2016, NIST published the first three post-quantum standards in August 2024, with more on the way:

ML-KEM is the one most people will meet first, because it replaces the key exchange in TLS. HQC was selected in March 2025 as a second KEM built on a different mathematical problem, so that a future break of lattices would not leave the world without a standard; its final standard is expected around 2027.

On the timeline, NIST's transition guidance (IR 8547) proposes deprecating RSA and elliptic-curve algorithms at the 112-bit security level after 2030 and disallowing them entirely after 2035.

How organizations should start migrating

From an attack surface perspective, cryptography is an asset like any other: you cannot protect, or migrate, what you have not inventoried. A realistic starting plan looks like this:

  1. Build a cryptographic inventory. Find where RSA, ECDH and ECDSA are used: TLS endpoints, VPNs, SSH, certificates and PKI, code signing, libraries and hardware such as HSMs and smart cards. External exposure (internet-facing TLS) is the easiest place to begin.
  2. Classify by data lifetime. Systems that protect data needing confidentiality for 10+ years go first, because of harvest-now-decrypt-later.
  3. Adopt hybrid key exchange. Combining a classical and a post-quantum algorithm (for example X25519 together with ML-KEM-768) means an attacker has to break both. Major browsers and CDNs already support hybrid key exchange in TLS 1.3, so this is deployable today.
  4. Design for crypto-agility. Avoid hard-coding algorithms. Systems that can swap primitives through configuration will survive both this migration and the next one.
  5. Ask vendors for their roadmap. Much of the cryptography in an organization lives in third-party products. Their PQC plans are part of your risk.
  6. Plan for size and performance. PQC keys and signatures are larger than elliptic-curve ones. Test the impact on handshakes, certificate chains, constrained devices and network middleboxes before rollout.

Conclusion

The quantum threat to cryptography is not a question of if but of when, and for long-lived data the "when" is effectively now. The good news is that the solution exists: standardized, peer-reviewed post-quantum algorithms that run on today's hardware. The hard part is no longer mathematics but engineering: knowing where your cryptography lives and being able to change it.
